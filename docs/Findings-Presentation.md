# Presentation: Does the Knowledge Graph Beat Regular Search?

*Slide-by-slide content for Yann's deck (audience: Jo, Stefan, Harsha). Each slide
has the on-screen bullets and a short "Say this" speaker note. Written in plain
language on purpose.*

---

## Slide 1 — Title

**Does a Knowledge Graph Beat Regular Search for Incident Questions?**
What we tested, what we found, and what it means

Yann · UPS Watson · [date]

*Say this:* "Today I'll walk through a test I ran to answer a simple question — is our knowledge graph actually better than plain search for the kind of questions we care about? And the answer is interesting."

---

## Slide 2 — The question we set out to answer

- We built a **knowledge graph** of our incidents (RCAs) and changes (CRs).
- The promise of the graph: it understands the **relationships** between documents — e.g. "this change *caused* that outage" — something plain search can't do.
- The real question: **does that promise actually hold?** Does the graph answer relationship questions better than plain search?

*Say this:* "Instead of assuming the graph is better, I decided to prove it — or disprove it — with a head-to-head test."

---

## Slide 3 — How we tested it

- Built **two systems** that answer the same questions:
  1. **Our graph system** — finds answers by *walking the relationships* between documents.
  2. **A plain "RAG" baseline** — finds answers by *text similarity only*, no graph.
- Built a **test set of 29 relationship questions** (e.g. "which change caused the Nov 10 outage?") with **hand-verified correct answers** — I read every document to confirm them.
- Ran both systems on the same questions, scored them the same way.

*Say this:* "It's a fair, apples-to-apples comparison: same questions, same scoring, the only difference is graph vs. no graph."

---

## Slide 4 — What is the "plain RAG" baseline?

- RAG is the **standard way** AI answers questions over documents.
- Step 1: find the documents most **similar** to the question.
- Step 2: hand those to the AI to write the answer.
- Its theoretical weakness: it finds documents by **words**, not by **relationships** — it can't tell that one document *caused* another.

*Say this:* "This is the thing our graph is supposed to beat — so it's the right baseline to measure against."

---

## Slide 5 — What we measured, and why

We scored both systems on three things:

1. **Did it find the right document?** (retrieval) — did it pull up the specific document needed to answer the question?
2. **Did it state the relationship correctly?** (answer quality) — did the answer actually express the connection?
3. **Did it avoid inventing fake connections?** (trustworthiness)

*Why these three:* the entire point of the graph is finding **connected** documents and stating **relationships** — so we measured exactly that.

*Say this:* "We didn't measure vague 'quality' — we measured the specific things the graph is supposed to be good at."

---

## Slide 6 — How the scoring worked (and one honesty note)

- **Metric 1 (find the right doc):** a simple, exact check — was the required document in what the system pulled up? **No AI, fully repeatable. This is our most trustworthy number.**
- **Metrics 2 & 3 (relationship + trust):** an AI judged whether the answer expressed the right connection. Useful, but **noisier and slightly biased**, so we treat it as a guide, not gospel.
- Important: the **correct answers were written by hand from reading the full documents** — *not* generated by either system. So nothing is graded against its own output.

*Say this:* "So when I show numbers, lean on the first one — 'did it find the right document' — because that one is an objective fact."

---

## Slide 7 — The results

| What we measured | Graph | Plain RAG |
|---|---|---|
| Found the right document | **62%** | **79%** |
| Stated the relationship correctly | 62% | 81% |
| Avoided fake connections | 90% | 90% |

➡️ **Plain RAG matched or beat the graph on everything.**

*Say this:* "This was surprising. The graph was supposed to win on relationships — and it didn't."

---

## Slide 8 — What the results mean

- The graph did **not** beat plain search at finding connected documents — it was actually **behind**.
- **One real bright spot:** the graph reliably *captures* the relationships — every connection we tested genuinely exists in the graph — and it doesn't invent fake ones.
- But "having the relationships stored" did **not** turn into "finding the right document better."

*Say this:* "The relationships are in the graph. The graph just isn't using them to win. So the next question is: why not?"

---

## Slide 9 — Why did plain RAG keep winning? (the core reason)

We dug in and found two things:
1. The relationships **are** in the graph (verified — every link exists as a connection).
2. But the graph's way of searching is **"goal-blind."**

**Analogy:**
- **Plain RAG is a search engine** — you describe what you want, it ranks everything by match, hands you the best. **It knows your destination.**
- **The graph wanders** from a starting point, following the "strongest" connections — but **with no destination in mind**. It ends up in the busiest neighborhood, not at the specific document you needed.

*Say this:* "The graph explores structure; it doesn't search for an answer. That's the heart of it."

---

## Slide 10 — Why "goal-blind" makes it *worse*, not equal

- Both systems **start** with the same similarity search.
- **But:** plain RAG keeps all **10** of its results focused on the question. The graph keeps only **3**, then fills the other **7** by wandering the connections — ignoring the question.
- So the graph **trades away** 7 question-focused documents for 7 "wandered-to" ones.
- On our data, the **question-focused ones were better** — so the trade lost.

*Say this:* "People assume it's 'search plus a graph bonus.' It's actually 'less search, replaced by wandering' — and that's why it can end up worse, not just equal."

---

## Slide 11 — The catch: our test was biased (toward RAG)

- To build the test, we wrote the correct answers by **reading the documents**.
- So every relationship we tested is one you **can find by reading**.
- But a relationship you can find by reading is also one **plain search can find**.
- The graph's *unique* advantage is relationships that are **NOT written down anywhere** — and we couldn't put those in the test, because we'd have no way to know they're true.

➡️ The test, by its nature, **favored the cases RAG is already good at.**

*Say this:* "This doesn't undo the result — it explains it. Our documents' relationships are mostly findable in the text, so both the test and RAG handle them well."

---

## Slide 12 — What this all means (honest takeaways)

1. ✅ The graph **reliably stores relationships and won't invent fake ones** — real, useful value.
2. ⚠️ But on our **current data**, it doesn't find documents better than plain search — because our corpus is **small (~290 docs) and well-written**, so similarity search already works.
3. 🔭 The graph's advantage would show up when **(a)** the data is much **bigger and messier** (similarity gets noisy), and **(b)** the important relationships are **not written down** (only an expert knows them).

*Say this:* "The graph isn't broken. Its advantage just doesn't get exercised on data this small and clean."

---

## Slide 13 — What we need going forward

1. **More documents** — a bigger, messier corpus is where the graph's advantage would actually appear (and lets us build a bigger, more trustworthy test).
2. **Expert-provided relationships** — get the UPS team to tell us connections the documents *don't* state outright. That's the **only** ground truth that can prove the graph does something search can't.
3. **Re-frame the graph's value** — lean into what it's genuinely good at: **trustworthy, traceable relationships and corpus-wide reporting/aggregation** — not raw document-finding.

*Say this:* "These three things turn 'the graph didn't win' into a concrete plan — and they're the specific asks I have for the team."

---

## Slide 14 — One-line summary

> We rigorously tested whether our knowledge graph beats plain search at relationship questions. **On our current data, it doesn't** — mostly because our documents are small and well-written enough that simple similarity already works, and our test could only include relationships that are written down. **The graph's real value is trustworthy, traceable relationships and reporting** — and its document-finding advantage needs a **bigger, messier dataset and expert-provided relationships** to prove out.

*Say this:* "So the headline isn't 'the graph failed' — it's 'we now know exactly what the graph is good for, and exactly what we'd need to make its bigger promise pay off.'"

## Optional backup slides (if they ask for detail)

- **Backup A — the diagnostics we ran:** we confirmed the relationships exist in the graph (not a build problem), confirmed the questions reach the graph's traversal (not a routing problem), and traced each failure (seeding / wandering / crowding). The cause was the goal-blind walk, not a bug.
- **Backup B — why the AI judge is "indicative":** the judge is the same model family that writes the answers, so it can be slightly self-favoring and varies run-to-run. That's why we anchor on the objective "did it find the right document" number.
- **Backup C — the metrics in one place:** retrieval (exact, no AI), relationship accuracy (AI-judged against a hand-written required relationship), false-link resistance (AI-judged on trick questions with no real link).
